Abstract
Objective  The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) has identified and articulated the rights of 
people with disabilities, mandating how these rights should be upheld and protected by countries. The objective of this article 
is to explore how these rights can be translated into action for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD).
Method  We conceptualize what human rights mean in the context of people with IDD, examine models of disability that 
have been utilized to provide services, and explore how mindfulness research and practices could be better aligned with the 
CRPD.
Results  We present five core principles of interdependent human rights of people with IDD and highlight five key challenges 
that hinder the realization of their fundamental rights and freedoms, which are intrinsic to the dignity of all human beings and 
are an essential component of a just and equitable society. We delve into the key Articles of the CRPD that underpin these 
rights and freedoms. Various models of services for people with IDD have been developed and implemented. We underscore 
the strengths and limitations of the medical, social, and biopsychosocial models, and provide an overview of the emergent 
human rights model that is based on the CRPD. Mindfulness is discussed in the context of other interventions as an adjunc­
tive treatment and a potential intervention that could address some concerns expressed in the CRPD.
Conclusions  Human rights, disability, and mindfulness are inextricably linked and could be strengthened to advance equity 
and full inclusion of people with IDD in our society. Impairment cannot be used as a basis for denying or restricting their 
rights. We need to promote the understanding that disability is an essential part of human diversity, and people with disabili­
ties contribute unique and crucial perspectives in a just and enlightened society.

Human Rights
Human rights underpin the fundamental rights and free­
doms of every individual regardless of their race, ethnicity, 
sexual orientation, gender identity, nationality, religion, or 
any other distinguishing characteristic, including all forms 
and aspects of disabilities. These rights are intrinsic to the 
dignity of every human being and are an essential compo­
nent of a just and equitable society. The concept of human 
rights originated in ancient times, with Code of Hammurabi 
from Babylonian legal document (1755 − 1750 BC) being 
one of earliest. Others followed, including the Magna Carta 
(1215), the English Bill of Rights (1689), the American 
Declaration of Independence (1776), and the French Decla­
ration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789), which 
codified individual liberty and limitations on government 
power. Much later, the Universal Declaration of Human 
Rights was adopted (1948) by the United Nations following 
the unthinkable horrors of World War II and the Holocaust 
(see Parmenter, 2023 for a brief history of the concept of 
human rights).
“Human rights” is a fairly amorphous term that has been 
conceptualized differently across countries and societies 
with different cultural and political orientations. However, 
there are five core principles that overlap across nations: 
(1) Human rights are universal and applicable to everyone 
regardless of personal characteristics; (2) These rights, in 
particular human dignity, are inalienable and not contingent 
on a person’s behavior or actions; (3) All such rights are 
indivisible, meaning that a person’s civil and political rights 
(e.g., freedom of speech and right to a fair trial) are inex­
tricably linked with economic, social, and cultural rights 
(e.g., right to healthcare and education); (4) These rights are 
interdependent in the sense that the fulfilment of a specific 
right is dependent on the fulfilment of other rights; and (5) 
In truly civilized societies, all human rights are guaranteed 
and non-discriminatory concerning a person’s characteris­
tics (e.g., race, ethnicity, faith, sexual orientation, and all 
other distinguishing characteristics).
In theory, human rights declarations codify universal 
entitlements for all people, regardless of their abilities, dis­
abilities, or characteristics. In practice, such entitlements are 
violated across all societies and countries, given political 
and economic realities, wars, effects of climate change, and 
a range of systemic discriminatory practices. For example, 
people with intellectual disabilities (ID), developmental 
disabilities (DD), and intellectual and developmental dis­
abilities (IDD) have been subjected to exclusion, neglect, 
and institutionalization, resulting in compromised qual­
ity of life (Cummins, 2016). Although significant progress 
has been evidenced in some countries, many challenges 
remain, including: (1) stigma and discrimination (e.g., 
discrimination in educational practices, hiring and work 
conditions in employment, and access to public services); 
(2) public education (e.g., inclusive education is still an 
aspiration in many countries, with limited access to quality 
education); (3) access to employment (e.g., finding suitable 
employment is difficult because employers discriminate and 
underestimate their abilities and capabilities in fulfilling 
work requirements); (4) disparities in healthcare (e.g., many 
healthcare services are inaccessible, inadequate, or discrim­
inatory) leading to compromised health and mental health 
outcomes; and (5) legal capacity (e.g., reduced opportuni­
ties for self-determination in decision-making robs them of 
autonomy and directly infringes on their right to make criti­
cal decisions that affect their life).
The United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons 
with Disabilities (CRPD; United Nations, 2006) has been 
ratified by many countries to safeguard the rights of people 
with IDD. Among the Articles of the CRPD (see Table 1), 
these five are probably most pertinent for safeguarding the 
rights of people with IDD in terms of treatment and care 
provisions:
(1)	 Article 12: Equal recognition before the law. The CRPD 
mandates that people with disabilities, which includes 
those with IDD, should have the legal capacity equal to 
those of people without disabilities. Article 12 asserts 
that irrespective of their level of cognitive functioning, 
people with IDD have the right not only to make legal, 
health, lifestyle, and other decisions but also to have 
those decisions respected. This requires a shift from 
the traditional guardianship and conservator models to 
supported participatory decision-making (Shogren & 
Singh, 2022).
(2)	 Article 15: Freedom from torture and cruel, inhuman, 
or degrading treatment or punishment. This Article 
forbids the use of treatments, interventions, and prac­
tices that can be construed as inhumane or degrading 
for people with IDD (see Nisbet & Weiss, 2021). This 
would include seclusion, restraints, aversive practices 
(e.g., electric shock, punishment, and certain psycho­
tropic medications which have historically been used as 
chemical restraints).
(3)	 Article 19: Living independently and being included in 
the community. People with IDD have the right to live 
and participate in the community, with choices that are 
available to everyone. They are entitled to choose their 
residence, with whom they wish to live, and the nature 
of their living arrangements. This requires access to a 
range of in-home and community support services that 
are responsive to their needs and would preempt social 
isolation or segregation in the community.

Disabilities have been classified in diverse ways based 
on agency criteria for understanding the nature of the dis­
abilities and the development of necessary supports and 
services. Frequently, disabilities are conceptually classified 
based on specific defining impairments. For example, they 
can be categorized as: (1) Physical disabilities, encompass­
ing impairments affecting mobility and bodily functions, 
such as cerebral palsy, loss of a body part, and paralysis; 
(2) Sensory disabilities, covering impairments in any of the 
five senses, such as deafness or hard of hearing and blind­
ness or low vision; (3) Cognitive disabilities, involving 
impairments in thinking, learning, and processing informa­
tion observed in autistic individuals, people with IDD, and 
those with specific learning disabilities; (4) Mental health 
disabilities, incorporating conditions listed in the Diagnos­
tic and statistical manual of mental disorders (DSM-5-TR; 
American Psychiatric Association, 2022); (5) Acquired dis­
abilities, resulting from injury, illness, or environmental 
factors, including traumatic brain injury and spinal cord 
injury, among others; (6) Invisible disabilities, stemming 
from hidden conditions leading to chronic pain, chronic 
fatigue syndrome, and some mental health conditions; and 
(7) Developmental disabilities, diagnosed before the age of 
18 years, encompassing lifelong conditions such as Down 
syndrome, fetal alcohol syndrome, and many others, with 
people with IDD falling into this classification.
The understanding of disability in the context of people 
with IDD has undergone significant evolution over time 
(Donoghue, 2003; Fitzgerald, 2006; Lo-Bianco & Shep­
pard-Jones, 2008), beginning with religious discourses and 
progressing to scientific conceptions (Humpage, 2007). Var­
ious models of care have been conceptualized and imple­
mented, often by professionals and advocates dedicated to 
the wellbeing of people with IDD (Brittain, 2004). Only 
recently people with IDD have begun actively advocating 
for a reevaluation of how society perceives disabled people 
and for the nature of services they require as full citizens. It 
is informative to briefly explore some of models of services 
to understand how they have shaped societal perceptions of 
people with IDD and how these perceptions have been, and 
continue to be, translated into interventions and services for 
them. In addition, the way disabilities are perceived influ­
ences people’s expectations and interactions with people 
with IDD (Barton, 2009), making it a crucial aspect of any 
discussion on human rights.
Medical Model. In this model, disability is perceived 
as a medical condition or ailment, placing medical profes­
sionals as the specialist experts responsible for treating and 
alleviating both the symptoms and the underlying diseases 
or disorders affecting people with IDD (McTigue, 2015). 
(5)	 Article 25: Health. People with IDD should have the 
right to the highest attainable standard of health. By 
implication, this Article focuses on the disparities in 
healthcare access and treatment that are often endured 
by those with IDD. This requires community health 
providers to be knowledgeable about ways to elicit 
treatment preferences of people with IDD and to co-
determine what treatment and intervention methods 
would work best for each person. It would require a 
change in attitude of the health care providers in terms 
of acquiring skills needed to provide the necessary 
treatment in a manner commensurate with the person’s 
expectations.
The CRPD provides a blueprint for nations to significantly 
improve the lives of people with IDD. It recognizes and rein­
forces their inherent dignity, autonomy, and full citizenship 
rights. It strongly advocates their legal rights, freedom from 
harmful treatments and interventions, their right to live and 
fully participate in community activities, inclusive educa­
tion, and to receive the same level of health care as everyone 
else in the community. Furthermore, it requires all nations to 
provide the necessary supports and services to translate the 
intent of the CRPD Articles into reality so that people with 
IDD can live with dignity and enjoy meaningful lives. The 
CRPD has been instrumental in producing many positive 
changes in the lives of some people with IDD. However, 
a systemwide transformation is yet to take place because 
it presents a complex and costly challenge to change the 
mindsets of politicians, administrators, service providers, 
and families to translate the philosophy and principles of 
the CRPD into living reality for all people with IDD.
Disability
Disability is a broad term used to describe impairments that 
people live with on a daily or intermittent basis. It has been 
defined in various ways, with the Centers for Disease Con­
trol and Prevention (2020) defining it as “. . any condition 
of the body or mind (impairment) that makes it more dif­
ficult for the person with the condition to do certain activi­
ties (activity limitation) and interact with the world around 
them (participation restrictions).” According to the World 
Health Organization (2001), disability has three dimen­
sions: (1) Impairment in body structure or function, or men­
tal functioning (e.g., scoliosis, loss of hearing, or memory); 
(2) Activity limitation (e.g., difficulty in walking, hearing, 
or seeing); and (3) Participation restrictions in normal daily 
activities (e.g., engaging in social and recreational activi­
ties). The CRPD emphasizes that “. . disability is an evolv­
ing concept and that disability results from the interaction

by not removing environmental barriers they face and by

lives of people with IDD can be greatly enhanced through 
political, economic, and social transformations of society 
itself.
A critical element of the social model is its complete 
separation of impairments from disability, a point that crit­
ics argue is a significant flaw. They contend that the experi­
ences of disabled people, influenced by their impairments, 
significantly affect their interaction with various aspects 
of society (Palmer & Harvey, 2012). Individual impair­
ment is an observable attribute of a disabled person and is 
intricately linked to their lived experience. Another critique 
centers on the social model’s failure to account for varia­
tions among disabled people, specifically overlooking the 
intersectionality of different oppressive forces such as race, 
ethnicity, gender, sexual orientation, and so on (Fitzgerald, 
2006; Reindal, 2008, 2009). Advocating for full societal 
accommodation of disabled people, without considering 
these intersecting factors, may not adequately capture the 
lived experience of disabled individuals who cannot escape 
discrimination related to these issues. From a social cre­
ationist perspective, societal prejudices may not merely be 
the collective beliefs of society, but a more insidious form 
of institutionalized discrimination (Oliver & Barnes, 2012). 
These and related criticisms of the social model have led 
to calls for re-examination and re-invigoration of the scope 
and applications of the model (Levitt, 2017; Riddle, 2020; 
Woods, 2017).
Biopsychosocial Model. This model, developed by Engel 
(1977), aimed to bridge the gap between the psychoana­
lytic and biological conceptualizations of mental disorders 
in psychiatry. Grounded in science, this model extended 
beyond the scientific realm to combat the dehumanization 
and disempowerment of individuals with mental disor­
ders through empathy and compassion in medical practice 
(Engel, 1980, 1988). This holistic approach to disability 
recognizes the intricate and dynamic interplay of biologi­
cal, psychological, and social factors that determine human 
functioning (Borrell-Carrió et al., 2004). Biological factors 
may encompass physical health, such as genetic predis­
positions, medical conditions, physical conditions. Aging, 
including age-related changes in the body, and gender, 
with biological differences impacting susceptibility to spe­
cific disabilities, are also considered. Psychological factors 
include mental health considerations affecting cognitive 
and emotional wellbeing, such as depression and anxiety. 
People are categorized as disabled if their impairment hin­
ders them from performing tasks considered “normal” in 
comparison to those without disabilities (Blustein, 2012). 
Viewing disability from a biological standpoint, the medical 
model sees it as a biological issue independent of external 
factors such as the environment, societal values, culture, or 
politics. Disability, according to this model, results from 
limited functioning due to bodily impairments, encompass­
ing issues related to body function, structure, and cognitive 
limitations stemming from physical, sensory, emotional, or 
cognitive challenges (Palmer & Harley, 2012). The medi­
cal model gave rise to the assumption that people with IDD 
could overcome or at least mitigate their disabilities only be 
engaging in the treatments and services provided by medi­
cal doctors and other healthcare professionals.
One major criticism of the medical model is that medical 
experts extended their influence beyond treating disabilities 
to having control or authority over the lives of people with 
IDD, shaping perceptions of how society values such people 
(Haegele & Hodge, 2016). Diagnoses and labels provided 
by medical personnel led to determinations of the services 
offered, their types, how they were provided, and the ben­
efits of such services, often overlooking the values, desires, 
and aspirations of people with IDD (Barton, 2009; Hump­
age, 2007). Another significant critique is that by associat­
ing disabled people with a sick role and framing disability 
in terms of deficits (Mitra, 2006), the medical model per­
petuated stereotypes portraying them as abnormal and fee­
ble. Consequently, this portrayal has influenced negative 
perceptions, and condescending attitudes and behaviors of 
society towards people with IDD (Goering, 2015). These 
considerations led to the development of social models of 
disability.
Social Model. The social model of disability, introduced 
by Oliver (1983) as an alternative to the prevailing medi­
cal model, has since seen various iterations (Mitra, 2006; 
Pfeiffer, 2001). The overarching social model posits that 
societal attitudes, discrimination, and exclusion are the pri­
mary contributors to the marginalization of disabled people, 
including those with IDD (Winance, 2016). According to 
this model, disabled people not face barriers in life due to 
their mental and physical impairments; rather, these chal­
lenges arise from environmental barriers imposed by soci­
etal, cultural, political, and attitudinal beliefs (DePoy & 
Gilson, 2015). In essence, disability can be significantly 
reduced or even eliminated if society provides the necessary 
supports and services (Bailey et al., 2015). In contrast to the 
medical model, the social model situates disability within 
the socioeconomic environment rather than within the indi­
vidual, recognizing impairments as a natural part of human 
diversity. Discrimination against disabled people is consid­
ered a violation of their human rights (Coles, 2001; Palmer

The details of the human rights model are expounded 
by Degener (2016a) and the following six propositions dif­
ferentiate it from the medical, social, and biopsychosocial 
models (Degener, 2016b, pp. 3–14):
(1)	 Disability is a social construct, but human rights do 
not necessitate a specific health or body status (i.e., the 
absence of impairment is not a prerequisite for human 
rights because impairment does not impede a person’s 
human rights capacity).
(2)	 Human rights encompass more than anti-discrimination 
(i.e., they include civil and political rights, as well as 
economic, social, and cultural rights).
(3)	 Impairment is recognized as human variation (i.e., dis­
abled people must confront the spectrum of pain and 
suffering, similar to everyone in society).
(4)	 Multiple discrimination and layers of identity must be 
acknowledged (i.e., identity politics should be a com­
ponent of disability policy, considering minority and 
cultural identification, including race, ethnicity, gen­
der, sexual orientation, and the various impairment 
categories).
(5)	 Prevention policy should be sensitive to human rights 
(i.e., prevention policy is seen as human rights protec­
tion for disabled persons).
(6)	 Poverty and disability are interrelated, but there is a 
roadmap for change (i.e., disability is a development 
issue covered in Article 11 of the CRPD, providing a 
pathway for change in the daily lives of disabled per­
sons as well as during natural disasters and humanitar­
ian emergencies).
The human rights model corresponds to explicit articles 
of CRPD. For instance, it emphasizes the importance of 
involving disabled persons in decision-making processes 
that affect their lives (Article 4) and advocates for the elimi­
nation of barriers in all areas of life, such as education, 
employment, transportation, and public services to ensure 
full participation and accessibility for disabled individuals 
(Article 9). Moreover, the human rights model of disability 
is already being used in community and research settings 
Coping strategies, resilience, and attitude towards one’s dis­

self-efficacy, are also part of the psychological dimension. 
Social factors encompass societal discrimination, reflect­
ing social attitudes and prejudices, as well as accessibility 
factors affecting functioning (e.g., accessible public spaces, 
transportation, assistive and prosthetic devices), and social 
support (e.g., from family, friends, and community). These 
three global factors are interrelated and interact dynami­
cally, with changes or disruptions in one factor influencing 
the others.
The biopsychosocial model provides a comprehen­
sive understanding of people with disabilities and offers a 
methodology for clinical intervention. While considered a 
strength by some, critics argue that its comprehensiveness 
makes it challenging in applying the model to community 
care for disabled people. Despite the existence of a biopsy­
chosocial formulation manual (Campbell & Rohrbaugh, 
2015), significant variations in its use across disabilities 
have been observed. This variability is expected given the 
wide range of disabilities evidenced in our community, 
but the disparities in its application across agencies for the 
same disability are somewhat disconcerting. Another criti­
cism is that, in certain healthcare and rehabilitation settings, 
the medical aspect of the model tends to overshadow the 
psychological and social components due to administration 
by medical personnel. At the same time, others argue that 
the psychological and social aspects of disabilities are often 
overemphasized without due consideration of biological 
factors. The precedence given to each component depends 
on the clinical strengths of professionals, highlighting the 
necessity of a strong treatment team leader to provide an 
integrative and nuanced understanding of each person’s dis­
ability. Moreover, some critics note a potential inadequacy 
in the model’s emphasis on structural factors in the com­
munity that impose difficulties for disabled people. These 
factors discriminatory policies, inaccessible infrastructure, 
social stigma, and limited resources and services for specific 
disabilities.

Mindfulness and mindfulness-based interventions have 
been extensively researched for their effectiveness in treat­
ing conditions arising from human disabilities, including 
physical, sensory, psychological, and cognitive disabilities 
(see Singh et al., 2024 for a conceptual review). Individuals 
with cognitive disabilities, such as those with IDD, often 
face above-average difficulty with mental tasks and may 
also contend with co-occurring physical and mental health 
issues. People with IDD encounter multidimensional chal­
lenges in everyday life, which can be attenuated with appro­
priate supports and services. One of these services includes 
mindfulness-based interventions, which have been shown to 
enhance their quality of life.
Mindfulness-based intervention can be utilized in two 
general ways with people with IDD. First, they can serve 
as adjuncts to an array of evidence-based therapeutic and 
educational interventions, including:
(1)	 Educational interventions (e.g., individualized educa­
tion plans in special and regular education).
(2)	 Behavioral interventions (e.g., applied behavior analy­
sis and positive behavior support).
(3)	 Speech and language therapy for those with communi­
cation difficulties.
(4)	 Occupational therapy for those who have difficulties 
with daily living skills,
(5)	 Cognitive rehabilitation interventions (e.g., cognitive 
training in functional skills and compensatory skills 
training),
(6)	 Assistive technologies (e.g., augmentative and alter­

(7)	 Vocational and life skills training (Singh et al., 2024).
Second, mindfulness-based programs can be employed as 
the primary intervention, either with their caregivers as

In sum, disability is a dynamic concept continually 
evolving and shaping societal attitudes, influencing policies, 
and impacting practices for all disabled people, including 
those with IDD. As knowledge and attitudes toward dis­
ability undergo transformations, existing models have been 
revised or new ones developed to incorporate new insights 
derived from philosophy, service practices, and outcome 
data reflecting changes in the quality of life for disabled 
people. In this context, the emergence of new models par­
allels the development of new theories in science (Kuhn, 
1996). Undoubtedly, the medical, social, biopsychosocial, 
and human rights models are not the last models of disabil­
ity; newer models and approaches are already emerging. For 
example, the social-ecological model (McLeroy et a., 1988) 
has recently been adapted within a human rights context to 
account for the adaptive behavior of people with IDD when 
responding to maladaptive environments (Jorgensen et al., 
2023).
Mindfulness
Mindfulness, in everyday parlance, means doing something 
consciously, carefully, or attentively. In terms of Buddhist 
teachings, mindfulness is a somewhat limited translation of 
the Pāli word sati, or its Sanskrit equivalent smṛiti. It has 
been variously defined by Buddha in his teachings and com­
mentaries by contemporary teachers and academics (see 
Amaro & Singh, 2021). The most cited definition of mind­
fulness is by Kabat-Zinn (2003, p. 145): “the awareness that 
emerges through paying attention on purpose, in the present

focused on analyzing the correlates, moderators, predictors, 
and outcomes of mindfulness-based interventions.
One of the key issues is to raise awareness within the 
mindfulness research community regarding the essential 
Articles of CRPD and the necessity for further research 
in these areas. Following this awareness, efforts should 
be directed towards developing a consensus on the key 
elements of each relevant CRPD Article, which can be 
operationalized and defined. This, in turn, will facilitate 
the establishment of standards and measurement criteria 
for assessing the degree of alignment of past, present, and 
future research with the CRPD Articles. Similar to existing 
criteria for assessing research quality (e.g., Cochrane inter­
vention reviews, Higgins et al., 2023), the establishment of 
quality standards for alignment with the Articles of CRPD is 
crucial for advancing research and practice in mindfulness.
Fully integrating the aims of the CRPD and mindfulness-
based interventions for people with IDD will require over­
coming intellectual and practical hurdles. An example of 
such challenges is determining how to develop and imple­
ment mindfulness practices for people with IDD who face 
the most significant impairments, particularly those at the 
severe and profound levels of functioning. Adhering to the 
principle Nihil de nobis, sine nobis (“Nothing about us with­
out us”) aiming for full participation and equal opportunity 
for, by, and with people with IDD who live with the high­
est levels of cognitive and physical disabilities, necessitates 
finding ways to provide care and services without being 
paternalistic and overprotective. Addressing the wants and 
needs of individuals with disabilities, especially those with 
severe impairments, to achieve a good quality of life may 
significantly differ from recommendations by profession­
als and what is offered by service providers. While this 
perspective might not yet be prominent in the awareness of 
mindfulness researchers, it could be beneficial to adopt a 
disability perspective and the human rights model of dis­
ability as part of the theoretical framework for obtaining 
informed consent, provision of mindfulness-based services, 
and in participatory research in mindfulness.
