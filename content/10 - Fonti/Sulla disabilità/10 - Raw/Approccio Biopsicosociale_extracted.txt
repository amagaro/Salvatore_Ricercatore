Large-scale and detailed analyses of activity in the United States (US) remain limited. In this work, we
leveraged the comprehensive wearable, demographic, and survey data from the All of Us Research
Program, the largest and most diverse population health study in the US to date, to apply and extend
the previous global ﬁndings on activity inequality within the context of the US. We found that daily
steps differed by sex at birth, age, body characteristics, geography, and built environment. Quantifying
activity inequality using the modiﬁed Gini index, we found a strong correlation with obesity prevalence
(R2 = 0.804) and a moderate correlation with perceived walkability (R2 = 0.426) and the activity gender
gap (R2 = 0.385). This study demonstrates the value of digital health technologies in exploring and
understanding public health practices while highlighting the need to examine complexities, including
biopsychosocial factors that may contribute to activity inequality.
While the health beneﬁts of engaging in physical activity are well-
recognized1, several studies have unveiled inequality in physical
activity based on gender or income2–4. Previously, insights into a
population’s physical activity levels were gathered primarily through
self-reported questionnaires. However, technological advancements
now enable physical activity monitoring using mobile and wearable
devices in free-living conditions. These streams of data gathered
continually on a daily or even minute-by-minute basis can be shared
and analyzed in aggregate to gain insights into the overall physical
activity levels of the population.
A recent large-scale digital health study on 717,527 smartphone users
across 111 countries demonstrated a correspondence between activity
inequality and obesity and a city’s walkability, with ﬁndings of gender dif-
ferences in activity and obesity5. Such ﬁndings, if found to be robust and
replicable within the population of the United States (US), can serve as a
quantiﬁable benchmark for assessing disparities in physical activity in the
US and their relation to sex, age, geographic location, built environment,
and other potential key inﬂuencing factors. Further, such measures can be
used as metrics of success in evaluating the effects of policy changes on
public health.
ActivityinequalitystudieshaveyettobereplicatedwithintheUSdueto
a lack of available digital health datafrom independent and disease-agnostic
studies. Large research initiatives across the US, such as the Behavioral Risk
Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS)6,7 and the National Health and Nutri-
tion Examination Survey (NHANES)8, have collected behavioral and
activity information using self-reported phone surveys and research-grade
activitymonitors. Yet, activity monitoring was only collected during a 7-day
monitoring period and is thus likely to suffer from high variance and noise.
To enable detailed exploration of US activity levels at the population scale,
large-scale digital monitoring data is critical.
The All of Us (AoU) Research Program, an initiative of the
National Institutes of Health (NIH) launched in 2018, strives to build
a robust ecosystem of communities and researchers to deliver the
largest and most diverse publicly-available biomedical dataset with a
focus on populations usually under-represented in biomedical
research in the US9. Speciﬁcally, the program accumulates multiple
streams of health-related information such as electronic health
records (EHRs), genomics, physical exam measures, participant sur-
veys, and digital monitoring data (e.g., Fitbit ﬁtness trackers) from
≥1 million Americans.
In this work, we sought to evaluate the utility of AoU data for repli-
cating two seminal digital health studies5,10 that demonstrated activity
inequality and its relationships to geographic and walkability measures,
which we derived from AoU’s Social Determinants of Health Survey11. We
also assessed the relationship between activity inequality and key health
outcomes (e.g., obesity).

Results
Overall participant characteristics
Of the 15,620 AoU participants with Fitbit data available at the time of our
analysis, we included 7403 participants with over 4.1 million days of
monitoring prior to the COVID-19 pandemic (January 2016 to December
2019) from 47 states, the District of Columbia, and other US Territories
(Flow diagram in Supplementary Fig. 1). The participants included in our
analytical cohort had a median age of 53 [IQR (37, 64)], a median BMI of
28.2 kg/m2 [IQR (24.5, 33.1)], and a daily steps average of 8330 (Table 1).
Nearly68%and81%oftheparticipantsincludedintheanalysiswereFemale
and White, respectively.
Variability in daily steps across US states
Based on average daily step counts across different US states between 2016
and 2019, we observed that AoU participants in cooler regions (i.e., average
temperature ≤60 °F) engaged in higher levels of physical activity compared
to those in warmer regions (average temperature >60 °F) (Fig. 1a). The ﬁve
most active states based on average daily steps were New York, Colorado,
Illinois, Massachusetts, and California (Fig. 1a). The ﬁve least active states
were Louisiana, Tennessee, Arizona, New Hampshire, and Texas. We also
examined activity intensity levels using deﬁnitions of ≥150 min of
moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity (MVPA) or ≥75 min of
vigorous-intensity aerobic physical activity (VPA) per week, respectively
(see Methods)1. In states with the warmer average temperatures, 28% of
people did not meet the recommended levels of MVPA or VPA, whereas
this percentage was around 10% less in regions with cooler temperatures
(Supplementary Fig. 3a).
To better characterize the differences in activity between relatively
“active” and “inactive” US states using daily steps, we compared the dis-
tribution of daily steps in the two most active states (New York and Illinois)
and the two least active states (Texas and Arizona) (of states with n > 100
participants). All states exhibited unimodal distributions (Fig. 1b), and the
modes (i.e., the peaks) were lower than the respective means (Fig. 1b). Such
positive skewness of the distributions suggests that the range of daily steps is
wider among the active individuals in each state. In addition, we found that
the mode of daily steps for Arizona (7196) was higher than in Texas (6839),
whereas the mean of daily steps in Arizona (7568) was lower than in Texas
(7648). The higher mode in Arizona compared to Texas indicates that the
distribution skewness varies across states and, hence, reinforcesthe utility of
activity inequality evaluation, which can uncover activity variance within a
region5.
We next compared the overall distribution of physical activity across
New York, Illinois, Texas, and Arizona while normalizing the distribution
by each state’s mode (i.e., the most common activity level), enabling a
comparison of how the activity is distributed within each state (Fig. 1c).
After normalization, we observed that 79% of the population falls within
50% of the mode (the region between the light gray vertical dashed lines for
all curves) in Arizona and 72% in Texas. This difference in the distribution
curves can be translated into a metric of activity inequality, which is mea-
sured by the Gini coefﬁcient of the daily steps (see Methods) and char-
acterizes the systematic difference level in physical activity. Quantifying our
visual inspection from Fig. 1c, activity inequality in Texas (0.223) is higher
than in Arizona (0.217) because Texas has a slightly more asymmetric
distribution. Interestingly, while New York was one of the most active states
and Arizona was one of the least active, the two states display a similar
distribution (Fig. 1c), indicating a similar variance of activity around the
most commonly achieved daily steps of each state.
Variability in the gender-activity gap across US states
We hypothesized that the skewness in each state’s activity distribution
(Fig. 1b, c) may be attributable to gender differences in activity levels given
the ﬁndings by Althoff et al.5. Across all populations, males had approxi-
mately1200moredailystepsonaveragethanfemales.Thedifferenceindaily
steps between males and females was statistically signiﬁcant in all states with
morethan20malesandfemaleseach(seeMethods;studentone-sidedt-test;
p-value < 0.05), except for Washington (Supplementary Fig. 5). We also
observed that these activity disparities increase as activity quantiles increase,
with the largest difference in daily steps between the most active males and
females (Fig. 2c). Comparing Texas and Arizona, the two least active states
from Fig. 1b, the disparity in typical activity levels (deﬁned by the mode)
between males and females was approximately 2.3 times greater in Texas
(2885steps) thaninArizona(1241steps) (Fig. 2a).InTexas,the distribution
of daily steps in males is right-skewed while the activity distribution of
females is left-skewed when compared to the state’s baseline activity
(Fig. 1b). Overall, not only do typical daily steps differ across states (Fig. 1a)
but so do the distributions of daily steps, which is attributable in part to the
underlying difference in activity levels between females and males. In fact,
38.5% of activity inequality in the US was explained by the activity differ-
ences between males and females (Fig. 2b). As we found between Texas and
Arizona, states with larger gaps in activity between males and females have
higher overall activity inequality.
Obesity and physical activity
Obese and overweight individuals took 1963 and 531 fewer daily steps
than those with normal body mass index (BMI), respectively. Consistent
with previous ﬁndings5, we found that the fraction of obese individuals
(BMI ≥30) in each state increases with higher activity inequality
(R2 = 0.639 [N = 18 states], R2 = 0.804 [excluding outliers: N = 16])
(Fig. 3a). With every 0.01 increase in the value of the activity inequality
metric, there is a 5% increase in the fraction obese. We further hypothe-
sized, based on prior global ﬁndings5, that this relationship may be due to
females displaying a more rapid increase in obesity with a decrease in daily
steps. While we found that obesity prevalence does indeed increase as
daily steps decrease for both genders, we saw no difference in the slope of
this relationship (Fig. 3b). Therefore, the higher obesity rate in states with
higher activity inequality cannot be entirely explained by the lower-
activity females as the obesity prevalence increases equally in both males
and females as average daily steps decrease.
Walkability, biopsychosocial factors, and physical activity
behaviors
Walkability is a commonly used metric for gaining insight into a built
environment, which may act as an extrinsic factor inﬂuencing physical
activity1,12. The perceived walkability of a neighborhood (see “Methods”),
whichcanrangefrom0to18,didnotdisplayastronglinearrelationshipwith
average daily steps (R2 = 0.0132) (Supplementary Fig. 6), but we observed a
moderate association between perceived walkability and activity inequality
(R2 = 0.4263, p-value < 0.01) (Fig. 4a). When average steps, by the time of
day,were examined acrossneighborhoodswith highest (14 to 18) and lowest
(0 to 4) perceived walkability scores, we observed high activity from 8 AM to
6 PM during weekdays (typical US business hours) and from 9 AM to 5 PM
on weekends, with a gradual decrease in activity throughout the day for both
weekdays and weekends (Fig. 4b). These trends are consistent with previous
ﬁndings5. We also found that the two peaks in steps that typically occur on
weekdays during commute hours exhibit distinctly higher step counts in
neighborhoods with high walkability (294 vs. 259 and 315 vs. 242; Fig. 4b).
The distribution of steps taken early in the morning was similar between
neighborhoods with high walkability (n = 5) and low walkability (n = 5), but
individuals in the high walkability neighborhoods had higher average steps
throughout the evening after 5 PM during weekdays. During weekends,
neighborhoodswithhighwalkabilitycontinuedtohaveahigheractivitylevel
after 10 AM as compared to neighborhoods with lower walkability.
The observation that certain times of day (e.g., during commute hours
on weekdays) have higher step counts in more walkable neighborhoods
points to the possibility that public transit and pedestrian infrastructures
may be drivers of physical activity. Given existing health disparities and the
established concept of activity inequality5,13,14, we sought to understand
whether the relationships we uncovered betweenthe built environment and
physical activity are consistent across demographic subgroups. To enable
this comparative analysis, we deﬁned a new metric: the walkability-activity

Discussion
In this study, we leveraged the unique AoU dataset to assess the replicability
of seminal research studies on population-scale physical activity and to
uncover new ﬁndings in this ﬁeld. We demonstrated variation in activity
levels across geographic regions and demographic subgroups. We also
assessed the translatability of a previously proposed activity inequality
metric5 to enable a better understanding of factors that may affect physical
activity and that can be used to support policy decisions such as investing in
infrastructure to improve walkability. Ourﬁndings provide a foundationfor
robust evaluation of physical activity across the United States by extracting
actionable insights from commercially available wearable device data.
Our replication efforts pointed to challenges in comparing results
across different population-level physical activity studies that use different
measurement modalities. We found that the AoU’s Fitbit-based population
mean (2016–2019) was 3560 daily steps greater than the smartphone-
measured population average reported by Althoff et al. (2013–2014)5. In
contrast, we found that the proportion of active individuals (meeting the
aerobicguidelineof≥150 min/weekofMVPAor≥75 min/weekofVPA1)in
each state was, on average, 25% higher than in the BRFSS study10, which
employed self-reports. These differences in ﬁndings point to the potential
role of the timing of the studies, assessment modalities (smartwatch vs.
smartphone vs. survey-based self-reports), and/or data processing methods.
It has been suggested that smartphone-based step counts tend to be lower
than smartwatch-basedstep counts15. These, combined with our usage of an
HR-based ﬁltering algorithm to minimize adherence bias, may explain why
average steps in AoU were higher than those reported by Althoff et al. for
US16. Separately, methods of self-reporting physical activity10 fail to capture
absolute levels of physical activity17,18 and may be susceptible to recall and
response biases. Also, while the details of Fitbit’s algorithm are proprietary,
Fitbit notes that both heart rate and step data are used to estimate metabolic
equivalents (METs) and that active minutes are earned when activities with
≥3 METs are recorded for ≥10 continuous minutes19. Previous validation
studies of Fitbit showed comparatively accurate estimates of sedentary
behaviorbutoverestimatedMVPAunderfree-livingconditions17.Together,
these points may explain why the proportion of active individuals1 was
higher in AoU as compared to BRFSS. The overall physical activity differ-
ences across these population-scale studies in the US highlight the need for
standardizedmethods(e.g.,measurementmodalityagreement,harmonized
data processing algorithms, consensus deﬁnitions, and metrics) to enable
both observational and comparative effectiveness studies whose results can
be used together to improve meta-analyses in this ﬁeld.
A relationship between activity inequality and obesity has been pre-
viously demonstrated across countries5, and we found that the same rela-
tionship held true across the AoU cohort. However, while sex differences
were previously demonstrated as a mediator of this relationship globally, we
did notﬁndthisto betruefortheAoUdata.IntheUS,wefound thatneither
overall obesity prevalence, which is also consistent with the NHANES
study20, nor its responsiveness (i.e., the amount of reduction in obesity rate)
to physical activity levels differs between males and females. Our study
suggests that other potential underlying factors such as social determinants
of health (e.g., healthcare access and quality, education access and quality,
social and community context, economic stability), lifestyle, and dietary
choices must be additionally explored to explain the relationship between
activity inequality and obesity prevalence. In addition, while the AoU
Research Program collected BMI through standardized in-clinic physical
measurements, Althoff et al. relied on self-reported height and weight to
calculate BMI5. Self-reported data may lead to an underestimation of BMI
compared to in-clinic measurements because individuals tend to

Walkability is an important factor considered in urban planning that is
closely related to quality of life, health beneﬁts, and sustainability. Previous
work demonstrated an inverse relationship between activity inequality and
walkability, as measured by WalkScore, which assesses macro-scale features
like amenities (e.g., parks and shopping centers) and pedestrian friendliness
(e.g., city block length, street intersection density) at the city level5,21. In our
study, we were unable to use WalkScore due to the absence of city-level
information in the AoU dataset. Instead, we evaluated micro-scale walk-
ability using the Physical Activity Neighborhood Environment Scale
(PANES), which captures individuals’ perceptions of their local environ-
ment. Despite these methodological differences, we found a similar rela-
tionship between activityinequalityand perceived walkability. This suggests
that policies and interventions aimed at both macro-level improvements
(e.g., enhancing street connectivity and transit systems) and micro-level
changes (e.g., improving neighborhood safety) could help reduce activity
inequality.
However, we also found that the rate of increase in physical activity
with improvement in walkability varies across BMI and age groups, which
may be attributed to various biopsychosocial factors that differ among these
groups. This suggests that while universal improvements in walkability can
broadly increase the overall PA level, policy decisions might need to be
tailored to maximize beneﬁts across different demographic strata and
improve activity inequality. Although macro- and micro-scale measures of
walkability are often correlated, they may affect different demographic
groups in distinct ways. If city-level information becomes available in the
AoUdataset,futurestudiesshouldinvestigatetheaddedvalueofmicro-level
walkability information, and whether one measure of walkability is more
sensitive to the built environment’s inﬂuence on physical activity within
certain demographics. This comparison could provide valuable insights for
improving the overall physicalactivity leveland reducing activityinequality.
As a quantiﬁable metric, activity inequality can systematically measure
the uneven distribution of physical activity within groups, gaining an
understanding of the extent of these disparities. Further, it provides a
baseline for evaluating the effectiveness of targeted interventions and poli-
cies over time. For instance, future researchers can explore how activity
inequality and its magnitude vary with changing temperatures and geo-
graphies,especiallyduringtherecordheatwavesthatoccurredacrosstheUS
inthesummerof2024.Thequantiﬁabilityofactivitydisparityalsoallowsfor
targeted and evidence-based interventions. Instead of adopting a one-size-
ﬁts-all approach, policymakers can use activity inequality as a metric to
allocate resources and interventions that address the speciﬁc needs of
communities or demographic groups experiencing the highest levels of
activity inequality. Overall, activity inequality serves as a data-driven

this study cohort (68.8% female, n = 5094) compared to the population and

demographic and contextual factors in analyses and point to the value of
additional future work exploring associations between physical activity or
activity inequality and external factors.

Additionally, our Fitbit dataset spanned three years (2016–2019), and
we assumed that participants did not relocate during this period and had no
signiﬁcant changes in their BMI, given that BMI was only measured once
per participant during the AoU data collection period (Supplementary
Fig. 7). Also, the AoU’s Social Determinants of Health (SDOH) survey we
used to calculate the perceived walkability scores was conducted two years
after the wearable monitoring period (November 2021- June 2022). We
assumed that perception of one’s neighborhood did not change signiﬁcantly
between the Fitbit observation period and the time the survey was taken.
Future research should account for how potential spatial and temporal
changes such as activity changes over time, geographic relocations (between
states, cities, or countries), and seasonal and behavioral variations in BMI
and activity, may impact the results of this study24.
Our study assessed physical activity mainly with average daily steps,
but future studies can leverage other metrics, such as fairly active minutes
and/or very active minutes obtained from Fitbit’s daily summary report. In
addition, while our study focused on obesity as the main health outcome of
interest, there are additional physical activity-related health outcomes that
remain to be explored. Finally, the Fitbit device model information was not
available on the Researcher Workbench at the time of analysis to enable
adjustment for possible differences in data generated by different Fitbit
device models. We attempted to minimize the impact of this limitation by
including participants’ activity data only if they had corresponding heart
rate data, thus generalizing the ﬁndings to Fitbit devices with heart rate
compatibility.
Despite these limitations, our study provides insights to advance
knowledge surrounding physical activity disparities in the US by leveraging
wearable device data. Our study builds upon and departs from prior activity
inequality studies in several innovative ways. We applied a heart rate-based
ﬁltering algorithm to minimize adherence bias and ensure high-quality
physical activity (i.e., daily step counts) data from commercial wearable
devices.Ourﬁndingsonvariationsindeﬁnitionsofactivityinequalityacross
the US further support the need for equitable interventions to promote
physical activity, which can, in turn, reduce the prevalence of obesity and
chronic diseases25. We assessed adapting a prior global analysis of activity
inequality5 to the US using the AoU data and found both consistencies and
departures with prior literature. While we found lower activity levels in
females compared to males and the association between activity inequality
and obesity to be true, we could not demonstrate the higher reduction in
obesity with an increase in steps in females. The differences in ﬁndings are
most likely due to the demographic differences between datasets and
the smaller variance in physical activity and obesity rate among states
(i.e., homogenous lifestyle) compared to countries. Future investigations of
robust activity inequality measures are needed to better capture activity
differences across the US, especially when the data may be imbalanced
(e.g., demographics are skewed toward one sex or age group), and to
establish a relationship to health outcomes.
Overall, this study explored activity behaviors and activity inequality in
the US, paving the way for future research that harnesses activity inequality
metrics derived from commercial wearable device data. The activity
inequality measure opens future paths for research to explore the intricate
connections between activity inequality, health outcomes, and targeted
interventions for the US. The research that is enabled by this study could
revolutionize how population-level physical activity interventions are
conceptualized and implemented in the future.

Study population
The initial participant cohort was deﬁned using the Cohort Builder tool on
the AoU Researcher Workbench and consisted of 15,620 individuals who
provided any Fitbit data (from Jan 2009 to Dec 2022) using their device.
While AoU participant enrollment began in May 2018, historic Fitbit data
before enrollment were made available. From this group, we selected 8886
participants who met the following criteria: had completed The Basics
survey at the time of enrollment (survey allowed partial responses), at least
one in-person physical measurement visit, provided any Fitbit activity and
heart rate intraday data between January 2016 and December 2019, and age
≥18 (Supplementary Fig. 1). After excluding participants who do not meet
the device adherence threshold (as deﬁned in Feature Selection and Data
Preprocessing) or participants who have exceedingly high average step
counts (i.e., outliers deﬁned by z-score>4), we performed all analyses with
7403 participants. Of note, although available with the Controlled Tier
dataset v7 (C2022Q4R11), we excluded any Fitbit data collected after the
year 2020 to minimize the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on activity
behavior27.
Feature selection and data processing
Demographic information, including date of birth, geolocation (state), and
gender (self-reported as sex assigned at birth), was obtained from the
responses to the basic survey at the time of enrollment. Body mass index
(BMI) was extracted from each participant’s initial in-person physical
measurement visit and was used to screen participants for obesity according
to the CDC guidelines28 (18.5 to <25: normal, 25.0 to <30: overweight, ≥30:
obesity). Further, in this dataset, to protect participant privacy, birth dates
were abstracted to year (i.e., set to the 15th of June of a participant’s birth
year)29. Each participant’s age was calculated relative to each of the data
points available in the daily activity summary dataset, which was later
averaged for the overall analysis. We also processed AoU’s Social Deter-
minants of Health (SDOH) survey that was launched in November 2021 to
obtain information on participants’ perceptions of their built environment.
Speciﬁcally, we focused on the eight questions derived from the Physical
Activity Neighborhood Environment Scale (PANES)30 (Supplementary
Table 1). When comparing our results by region, we followed the region
designations provided by the U.S. Census Bureau31.

Fitbit data included daily summaries of activity (e.g., active minutes
per day and total steps per day) and intraday data for heart rate and step
counts. To ensure the validity of our statistical analysis, we implemented a
heart rate ﬁltering algorithm to identify days with suitable device
adherence32. As Fitbit’s intraday heart rate data is reported every minute, we
aggregated all heart rate measurements from a given day per individual to
determine the daily adherence rate, which is calculated by dividing the total
number of heart rate measurements by the total number of minutes in a day
(1440 min). We then plotted the daily adherence rate (ranging from 0 to 1)
from all participants. From a visual inspection (Supplementary Fig. 2), we
selectedan adherence threshold (0.4) to label the valid daysto beincludedin
subsequent analyses. In selecting our optimal adherence threshold for the
heartrateﬁlteringalgorithm,wemainlyconsidered(1)minimizingdataloss
and conserving variance (i.e., the demographic distribution of participants)
and (2) providing comparative results to Althoff et al.’s study5.
From further observation, we identiﬁed unreasonably high daily step
values (i.e., daily steps ≥50,000 steps, which is more than would be expected
froma marathon run) from 172 unique individuals. We removed these days
(n = 1479), attributing such deviations to errors in the device or its data
processing. In addition, since the AoU database contains participants’ his-
torical Fitbit data from before their enrollment, any data points from before
the participant turned 18 in age were removed from the analysis. Using all
valid days, we calculated the average daily steps and active minutes for each
participant and used z-scores to exclude any individuals with extreme
outliers (n < 20), which are most likely due to having fewer valid days
(deﬁned as |z-score | > 4).
Lastly, an indicator of physical activity was derived based on the aerobic
guideline by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. As deﬁned
by the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, an active individual
engages in a minimum of 150 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity
(MVPA) or 75 min of vigorous physical activity (VPA) per week)1. While the
BRFSS10 determines whether individuals meet the aerobic activity guideline
based on self-reported physical activity frequency and durations per week,
here, we were able to leverage the “fairly active minutes” and “very active
minutes” from Fitbit’s daily summary report to derive weekly moderate-
intensity and vigorous-intensity activity minutes, respectively. We consider
an individual to have met the aerobic activity guideline if their fairly active
minutes + 2*(vigorous-intensity activity minutes) ≥150 min/week1.
Statistical analysis
To examine the relationship between activity inequality and various factors
such as geographic location, biological sex, obesity, and walkability (an
environmental factor), we used the methods described in Althoff et al.’s
paper. This includes the determination of the Gini coefﬁcient based on the
Lorenz curve drawn by Althoff et al., which plots the proportion of the total
daily steps of the population that arecumulatively recorded by the bottom X
% of the population, where X is some pre-set threshold value5. The Gini
coefﬁcientismathematicallycalculatedbydividingtheareathatliesbetween
the line of equality and the Lorenz curve by the total area under the line of
equality33. Thus, the Gini coefﬁcient can be interpreted as an indicator of
how dispersed the data is—values closer to 1 indicate higher inequality (or
variance) within the population.
To investigate the geographic disparity in activity, we created a forest
plot to visualize the mean of all individuals’ daily steps in each state, along
with a 90% conﬁdence interval (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 2). Only
states with >20 participants were included as per the data privacy and
protection guidelines from the AoU Researcher Workbench. We also
indicated the average annual temperature of each from January 2016 to
December 2019, as obtained from the NOAA National Centers for Envir-
onmental Information34. The activity trend was further compared to the
mean results of the phone surveys conducted by BRFSS in 2017 and 2019
(Supplementary Fig. 3). We also generated a continuous probability density
plot of daily steps for the two most active (New York and Illinois) and two
least active states (Texas and Arizona) with more than 150 participants
using the kernel density estimate (KDE) method35 (Fig. 1b). The bandwidth
wasselected usingScott’s rule asthe estimator and adjusted to smoothen the
kernel density function. In addition, we normalized the density plot to each
state’s mode (representative of the baseline activity of each state) to better
characterize the activity variance among individuals in each state (Fig. 1c).
The activity variance in a state was further investigated by plotting the
activity of males and females separately to assess whether the activity dif-
ferences across states (Fig. 1b) are related to sex assigned at birth (Fig. 2a).
The association between activity inequality and gaps in activity by sex at
birth, which is deﬁned by the ratio of average daily steps of females to the
difference in average daily steps between males and females, among states
was also examined (Fig. 2b). We also examined if the gaps in activity by sex
at birth differ depending on the typical physical activity level. We computed
the ﬁrst, second, and third quartiles of average steps per day in ehttps://doi.
org/10.1007/3-540-27661-0_4ach state (Fig. 2c).
For the analysis to understand the relationship between activity
inequalityandobesity,weonlyincludedstateswith>30participants(Fig.3a).
A greater number of participants per state (compared to Fig. 1a) ensured
sufﬁcient data points to compute the activity inequality measure. We ﬁt two
linear regression lines that minimize the residual sum of squares—one with
and one excluding outliers (Washington and Louisiana). We then investi-
gatedtheassociationbetweensexassignedatbirthandobesitybyplottingthe
obesityprevalence(fractionofthepopulationclassiﬁedasobese)ofeachstate
against their average daily steps (Fig. 3b). The obesity prevalence and daily
steps for each state were rounded to the nearest 1000.
Analysis of walkability and activity inequality was performed by
deﬁning “perceived walkability” from the relevant responses to AoU’s
SDOHsurvey(SupplementaryTable1).Outoftheeightquestionsthatwere
sourced fromPANES30,weeliminatedtwoquestions(RecreationalFacilities
and Day Safety) due to the low response rate (Supplementary Fig. 4). Apart
from the question regarding the main housing type in the neighborhood,
responses were transformed using a four-point scale, ranging from 0
(strongly disagree) to 3 (strongly agree), to gauge agreement with the
statements related to walkability features. The questions regarding housing
types were encoded on the same scale and by low (detached single-family
housing) to high residential density (apartments or condos) (Supplemen-
tary Table 1). Perceived walkability was obtained by summing the scores
from all six responses.
To determine the association of activity inequality with perceived
walkability, we grouped individuals based on the perceived walkability of
their neighborhood (Fig. 4a). To ensure statistical robustness, we excluded
perceived walkability groups with ≤20 individuals (n = 18 groups included,
n = 1 excluded). To explore activity trends in neighborhoods with high and
low perceived walkability, we plotted Fitbit intraday step count over the
course of the day. We binned the data into 30-min segments and averaged
the data in each bin by weekdays or weekends. We plotted these 30-min-
averaged steps for the ﬁve neighborhoods with the highest perceived
walkability scores and the ﬁve neighborhoods with the lowest perceived
walkability scores (Figs. 4b and Fig. 3c). We smoothed the curve by per-
formingamovingaverage(takingtheaverageof thelasttwo 30-minbins) to
better identify the diurnal trend. Finally, we examined the association
between perceived walkability and activity level in each of the following
subpopulations: sex assigned at birth, age, and obesity level (Fig. 4d). We
again excluded groups with ≤20 participants. From the groups that met
these criteria, we ﬁt a LOESS (locally estimated scatterplot smoothing)
regression model with perceived walkability score as the independent
variable and daily steps as the dependent variable for each subpopulation.
We compared the slopes, which represent the estimated increase in daily
steps for a one-point increase in perceived walkability score, across sub-
groups.Theparametersofthelinearregressionmodelforeachsubgroup are
also reported (Supplementary Table 3).
