Dynamic solar irradiance 
estimation for vehicle thermal 
management using a multi-modal 
machine learning framework
Rial A. Rajagukguk1, Hoseong Lee2 & Hyunjin Lee1
Accurate solar irradiance estimation for moving vehicles is essential for effective thermal management 
and energy optimization, particularly in electric vehicles (EVs) where cabin climate control directly 
impacts battery usage and driving range. However, existing approaches often overlook the dynamic 
environmental conditions encountered during vehicle movement, limiting their applicability in real-
world scenarios. This study proposes a novel machine learning framework that integrates satellite 
remote sensing and sky camera imagery to estimate solar irradiance around moving vehicles in real 
time. The framework was validated using comprehensive field data collected from an instrumented 
vehicle equipped with solar irradiance sensors, GPS tracking, and environmental monitoring systems 
across various routes and seasonal conditions. By fusing multi-source environmental data, the model 
delivers high spatiotemporal resolution predictions of solar irradiance under diverse urban and 
atmospheric conditions. Results show strong performance, with normalized root-mean-square errors 
of 14.61% in summer and 17.10% in winter. These accurate predictions enable adaptive, location-
aware thermal management strategies that proactively adjust climate control based on anticipated 
solar exposure. The integration of satellite and ground-based data sources ensures scalability across 
regions and vehicle types, establishing a foundation for intelligent thermal management systems that 
enhance energy efficiency and passenger comfort in both conventional and electric vehicles.
Keywords  Moving vehicles, Machine learning, Solar irradiance, Satellite data, Sky images

Vehicles represent a critical component of global transportation systems, and their interaction with the 
surrounding environment significantly impacts passenger comfort, energy efficiency, and system design. Among 
these environmental factors, solar irradiance plays a key role in influencing cabin thermal conditions1,2, especially 
when vehicles are exposed to direct sunlight. When parked or operated in sunny conditions, cabin temperatures 
can rise rapidly, leading to passenger discomfort and increased energy demand from cooling systems3. This issue 
is especially critical in hot climates or during summer months, where managing internal heat loads becomes 
essential for maintaining comfort and minimizing energy consumption.
Research highlights that 50% to 75% of the thermal energy accumulated inside a vehicle cabin is a result 
of solar energy transmitted and absorbed through the glass window4,5. The effect is particularly pronounced 
in modern vehicle designs, especially electric vehicles (EVs), which often feature larger glass areas such as 
panoramic roofs and wide windshields to enhance visibility and aesthetics. While these design choices improve 
occupant experience, they also increase the exposure to solar radiation, intensifying thermal gains during 
summer. However, during colder seasons, solar irradiance can act as a passive heat source, reducing reliance on 
electric or fuel-based heating systems6. This dual nature highlights the importance of accurately estimating solar 
irradiance to support intelligent climate control strategies in all vehicle types, with even greater importance for 
EVs, where cabin thermal management directly affects battery performance and driving range7.
For this reason, solar irradiance data is crucial for understanding and addressing the effects of solar 
heat gain inside a vehicle cabin. Accurate solar irradiance measurements enable manufacturers to develop 
effective solutions, such as advanced glazing materials8 and enhanced insulation, to minimize thermal loads9. 
Solar irradiance including direct and diffuse irradiance data also plays a critical role in predictive thermal 
modeling10,11,  enabling engineers to simulate how different vehicle designs will perform under various 
climate conditions before physical prototypes are built. Furthermore, accurate solar irradiance information is 
valuable for photovoltaic applications12,13, supporting the optimization of energy harvesting and integration 
of solar panels on vehicle surfaces, which can enhance overall vehicle efficiency. However, measuring these 
components typically involves equipment with moving parts, such as a sun tracker, which is challenging to 
install on a vehicle14,15, Therefore, as a practical alternative, solar irradiance models provide an effective solution 
for estimating these values without requiring complex instrumentation.
Recent advancements in satellite remote sensing technology have significantly improved the accuracy of solar 
irradiance estimation, offering high-resolution spatial and temporal coverage that is crucial for assessing solar 
energy potential across large areas16–18. However, while satellite imagery provides valuable data, it faces inherent 
limitations, especially in urban environments characterized by complex landscapes with buildings, trees, and 
other structures. These obstacles create significant challenges in accurately estimating solar irradiance from 
satellite data alone, as the presence of urban features can obstruct the line of sight between the satellite and the 
ground, leading to errors in irradiance predictions. In urban areas, the interaction between solar irradiance and 
these structures results in a complex interplay of shadowing, reflection, and scattering, which satellite sensors 
may struggle to capture effectively19. This is particularly true for solar irradiance that reaches the ground in the 
form of direct sunlight, which can be blocked or reflected by tall buildings and other infrastructure. Furthermore, 
the diffuse component of solar radiation, which scatters through the atmosphere and reaches the ground from 
various angles, is often challenging to estimate accurately from satellite data due to the variability in cloud cover, 
atmospheric conditions, and local geography.
To address these limitations, the integration of sky camera data with satellite imagery has emerged as a 
powerful solution. Sky cameras provide real-time visual information of the sky, enabling the identification of 
cloud cover, obstructions, and other atmospheric conditions that can directly impact solar irradiance reaching 
the surface. Unlike geographic information systems (GIS) and other static spatial models to estimate solar 
irradiance20, which rely on pre-existing maps and datasets, sky cameras offer continuous updates and can capture 
transient changes in the environment. This capability is particularly important because GIS-based models do not 
provide real-time information on newly constructed buildings21, temporary obstructions, or other structural 
changes that may alter solar exposure22. By incorporating sky camera data, solar irradiance estimation models 
can more accurately distinguish between direct and diffuse irradiance while also adapting to dynamic urban 
landscapes and evolving environmental conditions.
Recent research has investigated the integration of satellite remote sensing, ground-based sky imagery, and 
machine learning for solar irradiance estimation. For instance, Paletta et al.23 combined satellite data and sky 
camera observations within a single machine learning framework to enhance intra-hour irradiance forecasting 
(up to 60 min ahead). Similarly, Rodríguez-Benítez et al.24 integrated satellite and sky camera data to extend 
the temporal horizon of ASI-based nowcasting to 90  min. While these studies highlight the advantages of 
multi-source data fusion, they generally focus on fixed installations and do not address the specific challenges 
of estimating solar irradiance in dynamic urban environments. In contrast, our study focuses on irradiance 
estimation for moving vehicles, integrating satellite imagery, real-time sky camera data, and machine learning 
models.
In this study, we demonstrate the feasibility and accuracy of a novel model for estimating solar irradiance 
around moving vehicles. By integrating satellite remote sensing, sky camera observations, and machine learning

Data and instrument
Satellite data
This study primarily utilizes spectral satellite imagery from the GK2A satellite, which is equipped with an 
advanced meteorological imager (AMI)25. The AMI features sixteen channels categorized into four groups: 
visible, near-infrared, mid-wave infrared, and long-wave infrared. These channels cover wavelengths ranging 
from 0.431 μm to 13.39 μm, with spatial resolutions varying between 0.5 km and 2.0 km. The GK2A/AMI 
observation data can be classified into three types based on spatial coverage: full-disk, extended local area, and 
local area. For this research, we utilized full-disk satellite data with a spatial resolution of 0.5 km and a temporal 
resolution of 10 min. Furthermore, the GK2A satellite level 2 dataset ​(​h​t​t​p​s​:​/​/​w​w​w​.​d​a​t​a​.​g​o​.​k​r​/​d​a​t​a​/​1​5​1​3​9​4​5​0​/​o​

Sensors on car
In this experiment, we utilized the IONIQ-5 electric vehicle, which was equipped with an array of sensors 
mounted on its roof, including pyranometer, relative humidity sensor, temperature sensor, GPS sensor, and sky 
camera, as illustrated in Fig. 1. These sensors were employed to capture a comprehensive range of environmental 
data, enabling a thorough understanding of the conditions surrounding the vehicle during the experiment. The 
sky camera used in this research was the USB3 Vision industrial camera U3-3280CP Rev.2.2, equipped with an 
FE185 series super wide-angle fish-eye lens. This fish eye lens provides a 185° field of view and is compatible 
with 5-megapixel high-resolution sensors, allowing the capture of wide-angle sky images with a resolution of 
2272 × 1704 pixels, where each pixel value ranges from 0 to 255. Given that the vehicle’s location varies with each 
experiment, precise positional data is essential for correlating with satellite information. Therefore, a GPS sensor 
was included to obtain accurate measurements of latitude, longitude, altitude, and the vehicle’s direction. Further 
details regarding each sensor are provided in Table 1. Additionally, ground-based stationary data is incorporated 
for validation purposes. This dataset includes measurements of solar irradiance, ambient temperature, and 
humidity. The data is collected from a fixed monitoring station located at Kookmin University in Seoul, South 
Korea (Latitude: 37.611807, Longitude: 126.993986). To ensure temporal consistency between the mobile and 
satellite datasets, all sensor measurements from the vehicle were recorded at 10-minute intervals, matching the 
temporal resolution of the satellite data used in this study.

prediction studies. Data collection was conducted near the Han River along a 7-kilometer route, as illustrated in 
Fig. 2. The route includes diverse urban environments such as high-rise structures, shaded zones, and areas with 
reflective surfaces. The experiment was conducted during winter and summer, a period of low and high solar

Appendix-3
Tree-based machine learning models
Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)
XGBoost is an optimized implementation of the Gradient Boosting framework, designed to be highly efficient, 
flexible, and portable35. It builds decision trees sequentially, where each new tree attempts to correct the errors 
of the previous ones. The core idea is to minimize a custom regularized objective function, which balances 
model complexity and predictive power, making it less prone to overfitting. XGBoost introduces techniques like 
column subsampling, tree pruning, and parallel processing to enhance performance. It also supports handling 
missing values internally, making it a popular choice for structured/tabular data tasks like solar irradiance esti­
mation. One of XGBoost’s key features is its use of second-order Taylor expansion to optimize the loss function, 
which enables precise updates and efficient learning. It also includes regularization parameters that help control 
overfitting, a critical aspect when dealing with complex datasets. The use of histogram-based optimization fur­
ther speeds up tree construction by grouping continuous data into discrete bins. However, due to its complexity 
and memory usage, XGBoost can sometimes be slower for very large datasets compared to its competitors.
Light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM)
LightGBM is a gradient boosting framework designed to handle large datasets efficiently36. Unlike XGBoost, 
which grows trees level-wise (depth-first), LightGBM uses a leaf-wise growth strategy, where it expands the leaf 
with the maximum loss reduction. This approach leads to deeper trees with potentially higher accuracy. LightG­
BM also employs a histogram-based decision tree algorithm, which significantly reduces memory usage and 
speeds up computation, making it well-suited for large-scale solar irradiance data. A notable feature of LightG­
BM is its use of gradient-based one-side sampling (GOSS) and exclusive feature bundling (EFB). GOSS selec­
tively uses data instances with larger gradients to train trees, improving efficiency without sacrificing accuracy. 
EFB reduces dimensionality by bundling mutually exclusive features, allowing LightGBM to handle high-di­
mensional data more effectively than XGBoost. However, the aggressive leaf-wise approach can sometimes lead 
to overfitting if not properly regularized.
Gradient boosting with categorical features support (CatBoost)
CatBoost is a gradient boosting algorithm designed to handle categorical features directly without the need for 
one-hot encoding, which is a significant advantage for datasets with many categorical variables37. It employs an 
ordered boosting technique that reduces prediction bias by using permutations of the data during training. This 
approach helps prevent overfitting, making CatBoost a strong candidate for solar irradiance estimation tasks 
that might involve categorical data like cloud types or sensor locations. Another key feature of CatBoost is its 
ability to combat the gradient bias caused by target leakage using a novel method called ordered boosting. Unlike 
XGBoost and LightGBM, which can suffer from overfitting with categorical data, CatBoost’s native handling of 
these features ensures better generalization. While CatBoost is generally slower to train than LightGBM, it often 
achieves superior accuracy due to its advanced handling of categorical features and mitigation of overfitting 
risks.
